# Word and phrase tells — exhaustive reference

Load this during a detailed scrub (step 4 of the main workflow). None of these
words is forbidden in the abstract — any of them can be the right word in the
right place. They are *tells* because models reach for them by default, usually
to paper over a vague thought. So the rule is not "delete the word." The rule is:
**when you hit one, check whether it is standing in for something specific you
could say instead.** If yes, say the specific thing. If the word is genuinely the
precise one, keep it.

Scrub by rewriting the sentence, never by blind find-and-replace: a global swap
of "utilize" → "use" cleans one surface marker and leaves the vague sentence
around it intact, which is still a tell.

## 1. Inflated verbs and nouns

Models prefer the fancy latinate option where a researcher would use the plain
one. Watch for:

- *delve into* → examine, study, look at
- *leverage / harness / utilize* → use
- *underscore / highlight / underpin* → show, support, rest on
- *foster / garner / cultivate* → encourage, gain, build
- *showcase / demonstrate (as filler)* → show
- *navigate* (as metaphor for "deal with")
- *elucidate / illuminate* → explain, clarify, show
- *facilitate* → help, enable, allow
- *encompass* → include, cover
- *myriad / plethora / array of* → many, or the actual count

Inflated nouns: *realm, landscape, tapestry, paradigm, framework* (when there is
no actual framework), *ecosystem, arena, domain* (as filler), *lens* (as in
"through the lens of").

## 2. Empty intensifiers and significance words

These assert importance the prose has not earned. Replace with the evidence that
would make the point land, or cut.

- *crucial, pivotal, vital, essential, key, critical* (as applied to everything)
- *significant / significantly* without a number or test behind it
- *substantial, considerable, notable, remarkable, profound*
- *groundbreaking, revolutionary, transformative, game-changing, cutting-edge,
  state-of-the-art* (when not literally the SOTA comparison)
- *unprecedented, unparalleled*
- *rich, deep, powerful, compelling* (as unsupported praise)

## 3. Transition and discourse-marker overload

One or two are fine. A sentence-initial marker in paragraph after paragraph is a
rhythm tell. Vary them, or cut — often the logical relation is clear without the
word.

- *Moreover, Furthermore, Additionally, In addition*
- *Notably, Importantly, Significantly, Crucially* (sentence-initial)
- *Thus, Hence, Therefore, Consequently* (chained mechanically)
- *That said, Having said that, With that in mind*
- *First / Second / Third* used to list what are not really sequential points

## 4. Throat-clearing and hedging frames

Delete the frame; keep the content. The sentence almost always reads better
starting at the real subject.

- *It is important to note that …*
- *It is worth noting / mentioning that …*
- *It should be emphasized / highlighted that …*
- *One must consider that …*
- *It is widely / generally / commonly accepted that …* (name who, or cut)
- *In today's world / In the modern era / In recent years* (as openers)
- *As we can see, As mentioned above, As previously stated*

Reflexive uniform hedging — distinct from earned hedging:
- *may potentially, might possibly* (doubled hedges)
- *could arguably, it seems likely that, to some extent, in some sense*
- closing with *further research is needed* and nothing specific about what
  research, or what question it would answer

## 5. Formulaic constructions

- *Not only … but also …*
- *It's not just X — it's Y.* / *This isn't about X. It's about Y.*
- *From X to Y to Z* (the sweeping-range triple)
- *Whether … or …, one thing is clear:*
- *At its core / At its heart, X is about …*
- *The question is not whether … but how / when …*
- Rhetorical questions used to introduce a section ("But what does this mean for
  practitioners?").
- The tricolon reflex: three parallel items whenever one or two would do, and
  three-part parallel sentence structures repeated across a passage.

## 6. Promotional / editorializing vocabulary

Academic prose argues; it does not sell. Strip marketing register.

- *seamless, seamlessly, effortless, intuitive, user-friendly*
- *robust, scalable, powerful, flexible* (as unquantified praise)
- *comprehensive, holistic, end-to-end, all-encompassing*
- *meticulous, careful, rigorous* (asserting the quality instead of showing it)
- *exciting, promising, remarkable* findings (let the finding be exciting on its
  own terms)
- a *testament to*, a *beacon of*, *stands as*, *serves as a reminder*

## 7. Vague quantifiers and placeholders

Each of these marks a spot where a specific fact is missing. Fill it in, or flag
it — do not leave the vague version.

- *various, several, a number of, a range of, multiple* → the actual count/items
- *certain, some, many* (unquantified) researchers / studies / factors
- *recent studies / recent work / a growing body of literature* → the citations
- *often, frequently, typically, generally* → how often, under what conditions
- *significant improvement, substantial gains* → the magnitude
- *in certain settings / under some conditions* → which settings, which conditions

## 8. Structural and formatting tells

Mostly artifacts of models drafting in markdown and over-organizing.

- Bulleted lists where connected prose is expected (a Related Work section is
  paragraphs, not bullets).
- **Bold keyword:** label-style sentence openers scattered through prose.
- Headers and sub-headers for what should be a flowing argument.
- Emoji, or decorative symbols, anywhere in scholarly text.
- Suspiciously even sections — every subsection ~the same length regardless of
  how much there is to say.
- Em-dash overuse. (Em-dashes are legitimate and humans use them; the tell is the
  *rate* — several per paragraph, every paragraph.)
- A tidy summary sentence at the end of every paragraph restating the paragraph.

## 9. Domain-specific caution

Conventions differ by field, and some "tells" are normal in some venues:

- Humanities and theory writing is more discursive and uses more of the
  "landscape/lens" vocabulary legitimately — do not strip it into terse STEM
  prose.
- STEM results sections are terse and number-dense; discursive framing there
  reads as padding.
- Some fields (e.g. medicine, psychology) have required structured hedging and
  standard phrasings in methods/limitations — that structure is a convention,
  not a tell.
- High-prestige venues in many fields tolerate, even expect, a stronger and more
  opinionated framing than a model defaults to. Blunt is often correct.

When unsure which conventions apply, match the user's target venue or an exemplar
paper they name, rather than applying this list mechanically.
